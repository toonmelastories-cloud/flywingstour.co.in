The Letter of Guarantee, Explained

This is a document issued by a travel agent licensed in Egypt, vouching for the traveller as part of the entry process. Several independent travel-advisory sources describe it as effectively mandatory for Indian nationals applying for an Egyptian visa, a requirement that does not apply to many other nationalities under the same e-visa system. The practical implication: booking through an operator who can actually arrange this letter, rather than attempting a fully independent e-visa application, removes a genuine point of failure from the process.

Reported exemptions, for stays under a certain number of days, or for travellers under 15 or over 60, appear across some sources but are not confirmed by an official government page we could verify. Treat any such exemption as something to confirm directly with the Egyptian e-visa authority or the Indian Embassy in Cairo before assuming it applies to your specific trip, rather than relying on a single travel-agency claim.

The Rest of the Visa Process

  • The e-visa itself is applied for online, through the official Egyptian e-visa portal, allowing stays of up to 30 days
  • Your passport needs at least six months of validity from your date of entry
  • Visa-on-arrival eligibility for Indians is inconsistently reported, some sources describe it as available, others as strictly limited, so the e-visa route, applied for in advance, is the more reliable plan
  • Fee figures vary by source and should be confirmed on the official portal rather than from a third-party estimate

What the Cairo Leg Covers

The Great Pyramids of Giza and the Sphinx anchor any Cairo visit, genuinely worth a full morning rather than a rushed hour. The Egyptian Museum, or the newer Grand Egyptian Museum depending on what has opened by your travel dates, houses an extraordinary collection including Tutankhamun's treasures. Old Cairo's Coptic Christian quarter and the Khan el-Khalili bazaar round out a well-paced two to three days in the capital before heading south.

What the Nile Cruise Leg Covers

Most itineraries fly south to Aswan first, then cruise north toward Luxor, visiting temples along the way, Kom Ombo's riverside temple complex, Edfu's well-preserved Temple of Horus, and Esna, before arriving in Luxor. Luxor itself holds the Valley of the Kings, Karnak Temple, and Luxor Temple, widely considered among the most significant ancient sites anywhere in the world, genuinely worth the multi-day approach rather than a single rushed day trip.

The Red Sea Extension

For travellers with ten days or more, a Red Sea stop adds a genuinely different texture to the trip, beach and reef time after a history-heavy Cairo-and-Nile circuit. Hurghada and Sharm El Sheikh are the two most popular options, both offering strong snorkelling and diving given the Red Sea's clear water and reef systems. Sharm El Sheikh pairs more naturally with Cairo by a short flight than with the Upper Egypt route specifically, worth factoring into your routing if this extension is part of the plan. Dahab, quieter and more backpacker-oriented, suits travellers wanting a more laid-back coastal stop than the larger resort towns.

What to Eat

Egyptian cuisine blends Middle Eastern, Mediterranean, and North African influences, built around legumes, bread, and grilled meats.

  • Koshari, Egypt's unofficial national dish, a hearty mix of rice, lentils, pasta, and chickpeas topped with a spiced tomato sauce and fried onions, genuinely filling and widely available
  • Ful medames, slow-cooked fava beans, a breakfast staple found across the country
  • Falafel (ta'ameya), Egypt's version uses fava beans rather than chickpeas, giving it a distinct flavour and colour compared to the falafel found elsewhere
  • Grilled kofta and kebab, widely available and a reliable, familiar option for most travellers
  • Hibiscus tea (karkade), served hot or cold, a genuinely refreshing local specialty worth trying

Vegetarian travellers generally do well in Egypt, given koshari and ful medames' central role in everyday eating, though it helps to confirm ingredients at smaller local eateries where English menus are not guaranteed.

Etiquette and Practical Notes

  • Dress modestly, particularly at religious and historical sites, shoulders and knees covered is a sensible general rule even though Egypt's tourist areas are generally relaxed about dress.
  • Negotiate prices at markets and with informal vendors, bargaining is a normal part of shopping at places like Khan el-Khalili, and an initial asking price is rarely the final one.
  • Carry small denominations of local currency for tipping, tipping (baksheesh) is a genuinely embedded part of service culture across guides, drivers, and hotel staff.
  • Stay hydrated and pace outdoor sightseeing carefully, particularly around the pyramids and temple complexes, where shade is limited and the midday sun is intense for much of the year.
