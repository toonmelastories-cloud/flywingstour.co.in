The Two-Permit System, Explained Properly

The Standard Entry Permit

Applied for online in advance and issued on arrival at Paro International Airport by immigration authorities. This permit covers Thimphu, the capital, and Paro, where the airport sits and where the iconic Tiger's Nest monastery is located. For a short trip focused on just these two towns, this is genuinely all the paperwork you need.

The Route Permit, for Everywhere Else

If your itinerary includes Punakha, the former capital and home to one of Bhutan's most photographed dzongs, Bumthang's Buddhist heritage sites, or Phobjikha Valley's black-necked crane habitat, you need a separate Route Permit. It is free and typically issued within about an hour at the Department of Immigration office in Thimphu, but it is a step, not an automatic extension of your entry permit, and travellers who assume otherwise sometimes lose a planning day sorting it out once they realise.

The practical takeaway: if your Bhutan itinerary is more than a Thimphu-Paro loop, plan for the Route Permit as a deliberate first-day task in Thimphu, not an afterthought.

Getting There from Chandigarh

Only two airlines fly into Paro International Airport, Druk Air and Bhutan Airlines, and both operate primarily from Delhi, alongside Kolkata, Bagdogra, Guwahati, and Mumbai. Druk Air runs direct Delhi-Paro flights several times a week, a flight of just under three hours. From Chandigarh, that means a short domestic hop to Delhi followed by one direct international leg into Paro, no second connection required once you reach Delhi.

The Route

Paro, 1 to 2 Days

The Tiger's Nest monastery, Paro Taktsang, perched dramatically on a cliff face, is the single most recognisable image of Bhutan and the reason most visitors build in real time here rather than rushing through. The hike up is genuinely demanding, several hours round trip at altitude, worth pacing rather than treating as a quick stop.

Thimphu, 2 Days

The capital, and where your Route Permit gets sorted if your trip extends further. Beyond the paperwork, Thimphu's weekend market, the giant Buddha Dordenma statue overlooking the valley, and the National Memorial Chorten anchor a proper two-day stay.

Punakha, 1 to 2 Days (Requires Route Permit)

A dramatic mountain drive from Thimphu over the Dochula Pass, often lined with prayer flags and, on a clear day, Himalayan peak views. Punakha Dzong itself, set at the confluence of two rivers, is widely considered Bhutan's most beautiful fortress-monastery.

A Sample 6-Day Itinerary

  • Day 1: Arrive Paro, settle in, evening at leisure
  • Day 2: Tiger's Nest hike, a full day given the altitude and distance
  • Day 3: Drive to Thimphu, Route Permit processed, city sightseeing begins
  • Day 4: Full Thimphu day, weekend market, Buddha Dordenma, National Memorial Chorten
  • Day 5: Drive to Punakha via Dochula Pass, Punakha Dzong
  • Day 6: Return to Paro via Thimphu, departure

This pacing treats the Route Permit as a first-priority task on arrival in Thimphu specifically so it does not delay the Punakha leg later in the trip.

Extending Further: Bumthang and Phobjikha Valley

For travellers with ten days or more, Bhutan's central valleys reward the extra Route Permit-covered distance. Bumthang, considered the spiritual heartland of Bhutan, is home to some of the country's oldest and most significant Buddhist temples, a genuinely different, quieter pace than the Paro-Thimphu-Punakha circuit most first-time visitors stick to. Phobjikha Valley, a glacial valley that hosts endangered black-necked cranes migrating from Tibet each winter, offers a completely different landscape, wide, open, and pastoral rather than mountainous. Both require the Route Permit and add genuine driving time, worth it specifically for travellers who have already done the standard circuit or who have the days to spare on a first visit.

What to Eat

Bhutanese food is distinctly spicier than most visitors expect from a Himalayan cuisine, built around chillies treated as a vegetable rather than a garnish.

  • Ema datshi, Bhutan's national dish, chillies cooked in a thick cheese sauce, genuinely spicy and worth trying even for those cautious about heat, since it defines the local palate
  • Red rice, a Bhutanese staple, nuttier and denser than the white rice most Indian travellers are used to
  • Momos, Tibetan-influenced dumplings, found everywhere from street stalls to hotel restaurants
  • Suja (butter tea), salty rather than sweet, a genuine part of Bhutanese hospitality tradition worth trying at least once
  • Ara, a local rice or grain spirit, traditionally offered during festivals and family gatherings

Vegetarian travellers are generally well catered for, given Bhutan's strong Buddhist dietary tradition, though the spice level in most local dishes is worth flagging to your guide in advance if you prefer milder food.

Etiquette Notes

  • Dress modestly when visiting dzongs and monasteries, shoulders and knees covered, and a long scarf (kabney) is required for certain formal areas, usually provided or arranged by your guide.
  • Remove footwear before entering temple interiors, and photography is often restricted inside, worth checking with your guide at each specific site.
  • Bhutan operates a "high value, low volume" tourism philosophy deliberately, so expect a more structured, guided experience than a fully independent trip in most other countries, guides are effectively mandatory for most itineraries beyond Thimphu and Paro.

Bhutan for Family and Group Trips

The Thimphu-Paro-Punakha circuit suits families reasonably well, though the Tiger's Nest hike specifically involves genuine physical exertion worth weighing for older travellers or young children, a partial hike to the viewpoint café is a common compromise for anyone not attempting the full climb. For groups, the SDF's clear per-person, per-night structure makes budgeting genuinely predictable compared to destinations with more variable fee structures, and Route Permit processing scales reasonably well for group bookings handled through one operator rather than individually.

Booking Timing

The March-to-May and September-to-November windows are Bhutan's clear peak seasons, and hotel availability, particularly in Paro and Punakha, tightens accordingly. Booking two to three months ahead for either window is a sensible buffer, and slightly more for the autumn festival season specifically, since several of Bhutan's most significant tshechus draw both domestic and international visitors who plan well ahead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Indians need a visa for Bhutan?

No visa is required, but a valid passport (or voter ID card, OCI cards are not accepted) and an entry permit are both required, applied for online in advance and issued on arrival.

What is the difference between the Entry Permit and the Route Permit?

The standard Entry Permit covers Thimphu and Paro only. A separate, free Route Permit, obtained at the Department of Immigration office in Thimphu, is required for anywhere else, including Punakha, Bumthang, and Phobjikha Valley.

What is the Sustainable Development Fee?

A per-person, per-night government levy funding Bhutan's tourism and environmental programmes. Indian nationals pay a discounted rate of INR 1,200 per night, extended through September 2027, considerably less than the USD 100 per night rate other international visitors pay.

Is there a direct flight from Chandigarh to Bhutan?

Not from Chandigarh directly, but Delhi has direct Druk Air flights to Paro several times a week, a flight of just under three hours, so the trip is a short domestic hop to Delhi followed by one direct international leg.

How many days does a Bhutan trip need?

Six days covers Paro, Thimphu, and Punakha comfortably. Four days works for a Thimphu-Paro-only trip that skips the Route Permit process entirely.

Is the new GST charge included in package quotes?

It should be, and it is worth confirming explicitly with any operator, since the 5 percent tourism services GST is a genuinely new 2026 addition that an outdated quote template might not yet include.

Can I travel independently in Bhutan, or do I need a guide?

For destinations beyond Thimphu and Paro specifically, a guide is effectively required as part of standard Route Permit-linked travel, consistent with Bhutan's structured, low-volume tourism approach. This is not a bureaucratic inconvenience so much as how the country's tourism model is deliberately built.

Is Bhutan expensive compared to other Himalayan destinations?

The Sustainable Development Fee adds a predictable per-night cost most other Himalayan destinations do not have, but the discounted Indian rate keeps the overall trip broadly comparable to a mid-range Kashmir or Himachal holiday once flights and hotels are factored in together.
