Quick context: Uzbekistan requires a paid e-visa for Indian citizens, applied for entirely online at the official government portal, roughly 20 US dollars, typically approved within 3 to 4 working days. It is straightforward, but it is not free, and it is not visa-on-arrival by default.

The Four-City Relay

Uzbekistan's appeal is built around four historic Silk Road cities, and the country's layout means these are best travelled as a relay, one direction, rather than a hub-and-spoke pattern from a single base.

Stop One: Tashkent, 1 to 2 Days

The modern capital and your likely arrival point, Tashkent mixes Soviet-era architecture, including its striking metro stations that double as an unofficial art gallery, with the Chorsu Bazaar and a handful of surviving old-town madrasas. Most travellers treat it as a brief orientation stop before heading into the more historic cities, though the metro alone is worth a half day for architecture enthusiasts.

Stop Two: Samarkand, 2 Days

The single most essential stop on the entire route. Registan Square, three ornate madrasas covered in turquoise and blue tilework facing each other across a grand square, is one of the most photographed sights on the Silk Road and genuinely lives up to the images. Gur-e-Amir, the mausoleum of Timur (Tamerlane), and the Shah-i-Zinda necropolis, a corridor of intricately tiled tombs, round out a city that alone justifies the trip for a lot of first-time visitors.

Stop Three: Bukhara, 2 Days

A UNESCO World Heritage old town roughly 2,500 years old, and the most atmospheric of the four cities to simply walk through without a fixed itinerary. The Kalyan Minaret, the Ark Fortress, and a compact old-town core small enough to explore almost entirely on foot make Bukhara the city most travellers describe as their favourite once the trip is over, even though Samarkand tends to win on individual monuments.

Stop Four: Khiva, 1 to 2 Days

The furthest and most logistically demanding stop, a walled desert city and the best-preserved historic centre in the region, small enough to feel like an open-air museum. Given the distance involved, roughly 1,000 kilometres from Tashkent, Khiva is the city most commonly dropped from shorter itineraries, worth including only if your trip runs seven days or longer.

Extending Further: The Fergana Valley

For travellers with two weeks or more, the Fergana Valley, east of Tashkent, offers a genuinely different side of Uzbekistan from the four-city Silk Road relay. Known for its silk-weaving workshops, particularly in the town of Margilan, and its ceramics tradition in Rishtan, it is less visited than Samarkand or Bukhara and offers a more hands-on, craft-focused travel experience, watching silk being handwoven on traditional looms or ceramics thrown and painted by artisans continuing generations-old techniques. This is a genuine add-on for travellers with time to spare rather than a first-trip essential, but worth knowing about for anyone planning a longer, deeper Uzbekistan visit.

What to Eat

Uzbek cuisine reflects its Silk Road position, a genuine crossroads of Persian, Turkic, and Russian culinary influence.

  • Plov (osh), Uzbekistan's national dish, a rice pilaf cooked with meat, carrots, and onions, with regional variations across each of the four cities worth comparing as you travel
  • Shashlik, skewered grilled meat, a staple across every city and a reliable, familiar option for most travellers
  • Somsa, a baked pastry filled with meat or pumpkin, similar in spirit to a samosa and an easy, comforting snack between sightseeing stops
  • Non bread, a round, decoratively stamped flatbread baked in a clay oven, sold everywhere and central to Uzbek hospitality traditions
  • Green tea, served constantly and considered a hospitality ritual as much as a beverage, refused refills are considered mildly impolite in traditional settings

Vegetarian travellers should plan ahead, meat features heavily in traditional Uzbek cooking, though larger city restaurants in Tashkent and Samarkand increasingly offer vegetarian options, and it helps to flag dietary needs to your guide in advance, particularly in Khiva and Bukhara's smaller old-town eateries.

Who This Trip Genuinely Is Not For

Worth saying honestly. If beach time or resort infrastructure is the priority, Uzbekistan is landlocked and offers none of that, this is a history-and-architecture trip first and foremost. If a traveller wants minimal walking, several of the old-town cores, particularly Bukhara and Khiva, are best explored on foot over cobblestone streets, genuinely rewarding but not ideal for anyone with significant mobility concerns. And summer heat in Bukhara and Khiva specifically can be intense, worth avoiding for anyone particularly heat-sensitive. For travellers drawn to history, architecture, and a destination that still feels like a genuine discovery relative to more established international circuits, it delivers exactly what the current surge in interest suggests.

Packing and Practical Notes

  • Uzbek Som is the local currency; cards are increasingly accepted in Tashkent and Samarkand hotels but carry cash for smaller cities and local bazaars.
  • Dress modestly for mosque and madrasa visits, shoulders and knees covered, consistent with general Central Asian and Islamic cultural norms.
  • Comfortable walking shoes matter more here than almost any other destination we cover, given how much of the experience in Bukhara and Khiva specifically happens on foot over uneven old-town stone streets.
  • English is spoken reasonably well in tourist-facing roles in the four main cities, though Russian remains the more widely understood second language generally, worth knowing if you are travelling with someone who speaks it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Uzbekistan really visa-free for Indians?

No, this is a commonly repeated inaccuracy. Indian citizens need a standard paid e-visa, applied for entirely online, typically approved within 3 to 4 working days. Visa-free entry only applies to specific categories such as Indian nationals holding valid UAE residency, not the general tourist case.

Is there a direct flight from Chandigarh to Uzbekistan?

Not from Chandigarh directly, but Delhi has direct flights to Tashkent on both IndiGo and Uzbekistan Airways, taking under three hours, so the trip is a short domestic hop to Delhi followed by one of the shortest international legs available among comparable emerging destinations.

How many days does a Uzbekistan trip need?

Seven to eight days covers the full four-city relay, Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara, and Khiva, comfortably. Five to six days works well if you skip Khiva given its distance from the other three cities.

Which city should not be skipped if time is limited?

Samarkand. Registan Square alone is worth the trip for most first-time visitors, and it is the single most recognisable and photographed site anywhere on the Uzbekistan Silk Road route.

Is Uzbekistan safe for solo and women travellers?

Yes, Uzbekistan is frequently cited as one of the safer countries in the region for tourism, with low reported crime rates along the main Silk Road circuit and a well-established tourist infrastructure in all four cities.

Is Uzbekistan good for a honeymoon?

Yes, particularly Bukhara's walkable old town and Khiva's atmospheric walled city, both of which offer a slower, more intimate pace than Tashkent or Samarkand and have become an increasingly popular pick among Indian honeymooners drawn to the Silk Road's history and architecture.

Can vegetarian Indian travellers manage in Uzbekistan?

With some planning, yes, though it takes more effort than in a destination with a stronger vegetarian food culture. Larger city restaurants in Tashkent and Samarkand increasingly cater to vegetarian diets, and flagging the requirement to your guide in advance helps considerably in the smaller old-town eateries of Bukhara and Khiva.

Is the high-speed train between cities comfortable and worth using?

Yes, the Afrosiyob high-speed train connecting Tashkent and Samarkand in particular is modern, comfortable, and considerably faster than driving, and it has become a genuine part of the travel experience rather than just a transport link, worth booking in advance for the better seat classes during peak season.
