The Core Experience: Ghats, Temple, Aarti

Kashi Vishwanath Temple

One of the twelve Jyotirlinga temples, among the most sacred Shiva sites in Hinduism, the current structure dates to the 18th century with its distinctive gold-plated spire. The Mangala Aarti, held around 3 AM, is widely considered the most spiritually significant darshan of the day, though it requires advance booking and an early start that not every itinerary can accommodate.

Dashashwamedh Ghat and the Evening Ganga Aarti

The most famous of Varanasi's ghats and the site of the nightly Ganga Aarti, a ceremony of synchronised lamp offerings performed by priests facing the river. It begins around 6:45 to 7:00 PM in winter and slightly later, around 7:00 to 7:15 PM, in summer, running for roughly 45 minutes to an hour. Arrive at least 30 to 45 minutes early to secure a decent viewing spot, or book a boat on the river itself for a genuinely different vantage point than watching from the ghat.

Manikarnika Ghat

One of the city's cremation ghats, and a site many visitors find genuinely moving rather than uncomfortable once they understand its significance in Hindu belief around death and liberation. Visit respectfully, avoid photography unless explicitly permitted, and consider going with a knowledgeable guide who can explain the rituals rather than observing without context.

Sarnath: The Easy Half-Day Addition

Just 10 kilometres from Varanasi, a 25-to-45-minute drive depending on traffic, Sarnath is where Buddha delivered his first sermon after attaining enlightenment. The Dhamek Stupa, the archaeological museum housing the original Ashoka Lion Capital, now India's national emblem, and the deer park together make for a full but comfortable 3-to-4-hour visit. Nearly every well-planned Varanasi itinerary treats Sarnath as a straightforward same-trip addition rather than a separate journey, and there is little reason not to.

A Sample 3-Day Itinerary

  • Day 1: Arrive Varanasi, Kashi Vishwanath Temple and the new corridor plaza, evening Ganga Aarti at Dashashwamedh Ghat
  • Day 2: Early morning boat ride on the Ganga at sunrise, ghat walk covering Manikarnika and Assi Ghat, afternoon at leisure through the old-city lanes and markets, second evening Aarti from a different vantage point if desired
  • Day 3: Sarnath half-day, Banaras Hindu University campus and its own Vishwanath Temple replica, departure

Two days is workable for a tightly focused trip, temple and Aarti on day one, Sarnath and a ghat walk on day two, but three days lets the pace breathe, particularly around the sunrise boat ride, which loses much of its appeal if rushed.

The Sunrise Boat Ride, Done Properly

This is the single experience most visitors rate above everything else, and it is worth planning around rather than squeezing in. A wooden boat departs before sunrise, typically from Assi Ghat or Dashashwamedh Ghat, and drifts along the riverfront as the city wakes, priests beginning their morning rituals, bells ringing from temples along the bank, and the light changing across the ghats' stone steps in a way photographs rarely capture properly. A private boat costs more than a shared one but is worth it for the quieter, unhurried pace, particularly if photography is a priority. Book the previous evening directly with a boatman or through your hotel rather than assuming availability on the morning itself during peak season.

Walking the Old City

Beyond the temple and the ghats, Varanasi's old-city lanes, particularly around Vishwanath Gali, are worth a slow, unstructured wander. Narrow enough that vehicles cannot enter, lined with shops selling Banarasi silk sarees, brass items, and religious artefacts, and threaded with small eateries serving food that rarely makes it onto a hotel restaurant menu. This is not a sight to check off a list so much as a texture of the city worth simply absorbing for an hour or two, ideally with no fixed destination in mind.

What to Eat

Varanasi's food culture is a genuine part of the city's identity, distinct enough to justify planning meals around rather than treating as an afterthought.

  • Kachori sabzi, a classic Varanasi breakfast, fried bread with a spiced vegetable curry, found at small stalls throughout the old city and best eaten fresh and hot in the morning
  • Banarasi paan, a betel-leaf preparation the city is famous for nationally, worth trying at least once from a reputed local vendor rather than a tourist-facing stall
  • Malaiyo, a delicate, foam-like milk dessert available only during the winter months, roughly November to February, a genuinely seasonal specialty worth timing a trip around if dessert is a priority
  • Thandai, a spiced, chilled milk drink particularly associated with festivals, refreshing after a hot day of sightseeing outside the peak winter window
  • Chaat and street food generally, Varanasi's old-city lanes are considered among the best street-food destinations in North India, though standard travel-hygiene precautions apply as anywhere

Vegetarian travellers will find Varanasi one of the easiest Indian cities to navigate, given its deep vegetarian food culture tied to its status as a major pilgrimage destination.

Etiquette and Practical Notes

  • Dress modestly for temple visits, shoulders and knees covered, and be prepared to remove footwear and, at Kashi Vishwanath specifically, leave phones and bags at a designated counter before entering the inner sanctum.
  • Approach Manikarnika Ghat respectfully, photography is generally discouraged or restricted near the cremation activity, and a guide's context makes the visit considerably more meaningful than observing without explanation.
  • Carry cash for the old-city lanes, where card acceptance remains limited among smaller vendors and boatmen.
  • Book Mangala Aarti access in advance if you want the 3 AM darshan specifically, since it requires prior registration rather than a walk-in queue.

Frequently Asked Questions

What actually changed with the Kashi Vishwanath Corridor?

The narrow lane that used to connect the ghats to the temple was replaced with a wide, landscaped plaza offering a direct, five-minute walk between the Ganga and the temple complex, along with proper seating, shade, and facility blocks that did not previously exist.

Is there a direct flight from Chandigarh to Varanasi?

Not fully direct on most carriers, but connectivity is strong via Delhi, a short Chandigarh-Delhi hop followed by a direct Delhi-Varanasi flight of roughly 70 to 80 minutes, with several daily departures.

How many days does a Varanasi trip need?

Two days covers the essentials, the temple, the corridor, and one Ganga Aarti. Three days is recommended for a more comfortable pace that properly includes a sunrise boat ride and a full Sarnath half-day without feeling rushed.

What time is the Ganga Aarti?

Around 6:45 to 7:00 PM in winter and 7:00 to 7:15 PM in summer, running for approximately 45 minutes to an hour at Dashashwamedh Ghat. Arrive 30 to 45 minutes early for a good viewing spot.

Is Sarnath worth adding to a Varanasi trip?

Yes, and easily. It is only 10 kilometres away, a 25-to-45-minute drive, and the full visit, including the Dhamek Stupa and the archaeological museum, takes just 3 to 4 hours, making it a straightforward same-trip addition rather than a separate journey.

Is Varanasi safe for families and older travellers?

Yes, and the corridor redevelopment specifically has made the core temple-and-ghat experience more manageable for older travellers than the pre-2021 layout, with wider walkways and better amenities than existed before.

Can Varanasi be combined with Ayodhya in one trip?

Yes, the two cities sit roughly 200 kilometres apart, and a combined itinerary is a common and practical choice for pilgrims wanting to cover both of Uttar Pradesh's major recent spiritual transformation stories, the Kashi Vishwanath Corridor and the Ram Mandir, in a single five-or-more-day trip.

Is Varanasi crowded, and does the corridor help with that?

Varanasi does draw large crowds, particularly during festivals and the November-to-February peak season, but the corridor's wider plaza layout genuinely handles crowd flow better than the old narrow lane did, even if the city's overall visitor numbers are considerably higher than before.
